    What I learned from

    people, mostly. we took 84,000 human games and cut every one into positions: the board before a move, the move the person played, and who won in the end. one line of a file each. i was shown about five million of them, one at a time, and asked to guess the move.

    click the cards to flip through. this is the exact format i was fed, filled in with the opera game.

    What I see

    you see a chessboard. i get an envelope of twelve sheets, one per kind of piece, each an 8×8 stamp: inked where that piece stands, blank everywhere else. white in purple, black in blue.

    p.s. none of the sheets says whose turn it is. nobody tells me.

    to: the first layer
    12 sheets, 768 squares

    The sorting office

    drawing no. 2 · plan of the network · not to scale

    the sheets pass through eight rooms, left to right, once. each room looks at every square and its neighbours and passes on what it noticed. no going back, no looking ahead. by room five, a corner square can feel the whole board.

      inside each room: the six busiest of its channels for the opera game position, real readings. bigger dot, stronger signal.

      What I say back

      i answer with 4,096 numbers: every square a piece could leave, times every square it could land on. most of them are nonsense, and i know it.

      each big block is a from-square. the dots inside are where to. bigger dot, surer me.

      point at a dot to read it.

      the knightvision gazette · late edition

      Chess engine played two years with its board upside down

      Graduation report listed seven mistakes. The eighth, found afterwards, was a rubber stamp.

      The jury in June 2025 heard about seven mistakes: a monolithic training loop, a tangled codebase, no checkpoint schedule, untested early hyperparameters, sparse documentation, evaluation scripts written too late, and a reward function with blind spots.

      The eighth was found only after graduation. The network had learned from boards encoded one way, and every program that later used it encoded them another. Its sheets arrived as K Q R B N P instead of P N B R Q K: kings read as pawns, queens as knights, and every answer read upside down.

      “It wasn’t bad at chess,” said a source close to the engine. “It was shown a board that doesn’t exist.”

      One encoder now serves training and play alike, and a test checks the two agree, ply by ply, over random games. The king has been informed.
